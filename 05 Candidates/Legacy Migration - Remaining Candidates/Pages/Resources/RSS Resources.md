## Why RSS

RSS has become increasingly valuable as a way to maintain control over your information diet—curating sources without algorithmic filtering or surveillance. What follows is a living list of applications and services that make this possible. 

Most are straightforward tools. Some are excellent. A few have become indispensable for anyone who wants to stay informed on their own terms, not the terms of whatever platform owns your attention that week.

The readers below run the spectrum from native desktop apps to browser-based services. Pick based on how you work. The best RSS reader is the one you actually use - the one that disappears into your workflow and gets out of the way.

## Desktop & Native Apps

**Reeder** ([reederapp.com](https://reederapp.com/)) remains a popular cross-platform choice, offering a clean interface with solid feature parity across devices.

**NetNewsWire** ([netnewswire.com](https://netnewswire.com/)) is the gold standard for Mac and iOS users—open source with deep platform integration.

**Newsify** ([newsify.co](https://newsify.co/)) and **Unread** ([goldenhillsoftware.com/unread](https://www.goldenhillsoftware.com/unread/)) serve the iOS ecosystem with thoughtful design and strong performance.

**Stream** is a lightweight iOS-only reader that prioritizes simplicity.

## Web-Based Services

**Feedly** ([feedly.com](https://www.feedly.com/)) dominates the space—most widely adopted, straightforward, reliable infrastructure. No surprises.

**Inoreader** ([inoreader.com](https://www.inoreader.com/)) offers sophisticated search and archiving capabilities if you treat RSS as a personal archive.

**NewsBlur** ([newsblur.com](https://newsblur.com/)) brings AI filtering and more opinionated curation than pure aggregation.

**Feedbin** ([feedbin.com](https://feedbin.com/)), **Feeder** ([feeder.co](https://feeder.co/)), and **The Old Reader** ([theoldreader.com](https://theoldreader.com/)) all offer solid multi-platform access.

**FeedSpot** ([feedspot.com](https://www.feedspot.com/)), **RSS.app** ([rss.app](https://rss.app/)), and **Feedreader** ([feedreader.com](https://www.feedreader.com/)) round out the landscape for different use cases.

**lire** ([lire.app](https://lire.app/)) is the privacy-conscious, open-source alternative if you're building infrastructure around your feeds.

## Notable Platforms

**Feedland** ([feedland.com](https://feedland.com)) deserves mention as a distinct approach to feed aggregation—worth exploring if you're looking beyond the standard reader model.

**Miniroll** ([chrishannah.me/miniroll-global-feed](https://chrishannah.me/miniroll-global-feed/)) offers a global feed approach for discovery.
## Selection Heuristics

Choose based on your primary use case: do you want native app experience (NetNewsWire, Reeder) or web-based flexibility (Feedly, Inoreader)? How important is curation and filtering vs. pure aggregation? Do you need archiving and search (Inoreader, Feedbin) or just consumption (Newsify, Stream)?

Most readers offer free tiers. Test multiple and stick with the one that stays invisible—the sign of a tool that works.
## The Unsung Hero Status

RSS almost died. It didn't. Here's why it matters.

In 2002, Dave Winer released RSS 2.0. Before that, Netscape had cobbled together RSS 0.9, but Winer made it complete. He added the 'enclosure'"' element in 2001, which let feeds carry audio files. That single feature became **the foundation for podcasting**. Without RSS enclosures, podcasts as we know them don't exist.

**The critical part:** RSS is de-centralized. **You own your feed.** Your audience subscribes directly - no algorithm, no platform. Publishers distribute. Readers pull. No middleman.

Facebook used to publish RSS feeds for pages. Then they killed it. Why? Control. If your content lives in RSS, readers pull it from anywhere - other platforms, apps, aggregators. Facebook needed you locked inside scrolling their curated feed so they could inject ads and track you. They couldn't afford the exit route RSS represented.

You can still post RSS feeds *into* Facebook. The content flows inbound from the open web. But it doesn't flow out. **This asymmetry is the whole story.**

RSS is making a comeback not because algorithms failed (though they did), but because people want the feed back. Want the choice back. Want the ownership back.

**That's RSS. Unglamorous. Powerful. Still here.**
