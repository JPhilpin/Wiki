Ted Levitt was a Harvard Business School professor and one of the most influential thinkers in the history of marketing. His 1960 article *Marketing Myopia* is one of the best-selling Harvard Business Review reprints ever published, and its central argument remains as sharp today as when he wrote it.

## Marketing Myopia

The argument is deceptively simple: companies fail not because they run out of customers, but because they define their business too narrowly.

The railroad companies didn't decline because people stopped needing to travel. They declined because they thought they were in the railroad business rather than the transportation business. Hollywood didn't struggle because people stopped wanting entertainment — it struggled because it thought it was in the movie business rather than the entertainment business.

**The question is never: what do we sell? The question is: what problem do we solve?**

This distinction — between product and purpose, between feature and function — is the difference between building a business that compounds and building one that gets disrupted.

## The Relevance to Structured Thinking

Levitt's myopia diagnosis maps directly onto the [[Systems of Record]] vs [[Systems of Engagement]] distinction. Organisations that define themselves by their systems — their tools, their processes, their technology — are committing exactly the error Levitt described. The system is not the business. The outcome the system enables is the business.

The [[Business Equation]] asks the same question Levitt asked: what is the actual demand you are serving? Not the product. Not the process. The demand.

## On Globalisation

Levitt's 1983 HBR article *The Globalisation of Markets* was equally provocative — arguing that technology was homogenising consumer preferences worldwide and that the future belonged to companies that treated the world as a single market rather than a collection of local ones. The argument was contested then and remains so, but it forced a generation of business leaders to think about scale and standardisation in new ways.
