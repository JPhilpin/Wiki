Brand builds containers and lets other people fill them. The Whole Earth Catalog reviewed tools it did not make. The WELL hosted conversations it did not start. SALT hosted lectures it did not give. Each lasted because a slow, stable structure carried fast-moving content - the logic of his own Pace Layers model, applied to his career.

## Pace Layers

His best-known idea describes a civilisation as a stack of layers moving at different speeds: fashion moves fast, governance slowly, nature barely at all. It grew from the architect Frank Duffy's layers of a building, which Brand expanded in *How Buildings Learn*, and took its civilisation form in a 1996 sketch made after working it through with Brian Eno. [[Paul Saffo]] uses and teaches it. The 2018 essay "Pace Layering: How Complex Systems Learn and Keep Learning" proposes six levels of pace and size. Its central claim is that the contradictions between layers are where a system finds its health, because each layer corrects the others. The full model, with the six layers, is on the [[Pace Layers]] page.

The model explains why organisations struggle to change. Different parts of them run at incompatible speeds, and most change programmes try to move everything at once.

## What he built

| Years | Project | What it was |
|---|---|---|
| 1966 | Trips Festival | Three-day multimedia event in San Francisco, attended by about 10,000 people. Widely credited with launching the city's psychedelic counterculture. |
| 1968 to 1972 | Whole Earth Catalog | Oversized reviews of tools, books and ideas for self-directed living. The Last Whole Earth Catalog (1971) won the National Book Award in Contemporary Affairs and sold 1.5 million copies. |
| 1972 to 2003 | Point Foundation | The nonprofit behind every Whole Earth activity. It gave away $1 million in its first three years. |
| 1974 to 1985 | CoEvolution Quarterly | The Catalog's spirit in essay form, with contributors such as Gregory Bateson, Wendell Berry, Ursula K. Le Guin and Amory Lovins. |
| 1984 | Hackers Conference | The first gathering of hackers as a self-identified community, where Brand first said that information wants to be free. Annual since 1986. |
| 1985 | The WELL | One of the earliest online communities, started with Larry Brilliant. Now owned by its members. |
| 1988 to 2013 | Global Business Network | Scenario-planning consultancy co-founded with Peter Schwartz and others. About 90 multinationals as clients. Later acquired by Monitor Group, then Deloitte. |
| 1996 | The Long Now Foundation | Co-founded with Danny Hillis to think and act on a 10,000 year horizon. Home of the 10,000 Year Clock, being built inside a mountain in West Texas. |
| 2001 | Long Bets | Public, accountable predictions with a charity stake, co-founded with Kevin Kelly. |
| 2003 to 2019 | SALT | Monthly Seminars About Long-term Thinking in San Francisco. Brand curated them for sixteen years and wrote a summary of each talk. Now run by The Long Now as Long Now Talks, with more than 400 speakers. |
| 2012 to present | Revive & Restore | Co-founded with Ryan Phelan. Applies biotechnology to genetic rescue and de-extinction, including woolly mammoth and passenger pigeon projects. His 2013 TED talk on the subject was widely viewed. |

## Books and writing

*Maintenance: Of Everything, Part One* (Stripe Press, 2026) is his first major book in seventeen years. It opens with the 1968 Golden Globe solo sailboat race as a contest of maintenance styles, then moves through corrosion, precision manufacturing, the evolution of manuals and military sustainment. The argument: taking responsibility for what already exists, whether a motorcycle, a monument or the planet, is a radical act. Part Two is planned for 2027.

Earlier books include *How Buildings Learn* (1994, also a six-part BBC series in 1997 with music by Brian Eno), *The Clock of the Long Now* (1999), *Whole Earth Discipline* (2010) and *The SALT Summaries* (2011).

Two recent essays carry the same thread. "The Essential Art of Civilization" (2025) argues that maintenance is tedious, so people defer it, and that responsible maintenance is essential to long-term survival. "Elements of a Durable Civilization" (2024) asks what it takes to move from a global civilisation to a planetary one, and argues that the real work is managing continuity rather than predicting collapse.
